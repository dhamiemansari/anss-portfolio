Taking a bank’s business-loan application fully digital

Mobile and desktop onboarding for MSME business loans — from a link in an SMS to an e-signed application — designed in a month and now live.

← Go back

First, a little context

About the client

A private-sector Indian bank (under NDA) that lends to small and medium businesses — MSMEs. Until this project, a business owner who wanted a loan had one route: the call centre. An agent would call, collect details, chase documents and pass the application along.

Overview

The bank wanted a digital route a business owner could finish on their phone, without losing any of the checks a loan needs — from a link in an SMS to an e-signed application, on mobile web and desktop, with relationship managers following every application.

My role

Sole product designer — from the first wireframe to dev handoff. Flows, UI and specs across the applicant journey and the RM dashboard.

Timeline & status

1 month, 4 sprints. Mobile web, desktop and RM dashboard. Live in production.

3 user types

Applicant, guarantor and RM in one journey

A loan application is not an account opening

It needs the business too: registration, bank statements, existing loans, guarantors, declarations, a fee and a signature from everyone involved. And the journey had to work for three kinds of applicants, each with a different amount of patience and context.

Two more people sit around that journey. Guarantors, who never started the application but must verify and sign, and relationship managers (RMs), who follow every application and ask for corrections.

The kickoff handed me the journey as a list of integrations

Each integration came with a constraint the applicant would feel. None of them could be designed away — so the work was making each rule feel obvious instead of bureaucratic.

CORE PROBLEM STATEMENT

“How might a business owner finish a regulated, multi-party loan application on their phone — without calling anyone, retyping what the bank can already verify, or starting over when one thing is wrong?”

Synthesised from the bank’s brief, the integration list and the call-centre process it replaces.

Before drawing screens, I wrote down three rules every step had to pass

The constraints decided what each step had to do. These principles decided how it should feel to the person doing it.

01

Ready before you start

Tell people every document they’ll need before step one, so nobody stalls halfway.

02

Verify once, never retype

Every verified source fills in fields the applicant would otherwise type.

03

Every section fixable

Applicants and RMs work section by section, so one mistake never means starting again.

Breaking down the final design piece by piece

What shipped is a 13-step journey on mobile web and desktop. Here are the five decisions that did the most work.

1. One code, two places made the split OTP feel like one step

  • Problem identified: The rule sends the first 3 digits to the mobile and the last 3 to email. Two screens would have suggested two codes.
  • What I did: Kept it to one input: six boxes in two groups of three, with a gap where the code splits. The line above says exactly where each half went.
  • Why it mattered: It was the step most likely to confuse people — the split stays compliant but becomes visible instead of mysterious.

2. Verified, not typed — business details arrive on their own

  • Problem identified: Owners were being asked to type details the bank had just verified.
  • What I did: Once the business is verified, its fields come pre-filled and locked. A “same as office address” checkbox removes the next round of retyping.
  • Where it landed: The bank already trusts that data, and the applicant never types it twice.

KEY POINT

Trade-off: a locked field can’t be corrected in place. If the source is wrong, it’s fixed at the source — so the screen explains that instead of offering an edit button.

3. Proof of presence, and a separate door for guarantors

  • Problem identified: The live selfie is a fraud check, but to the applicant it’s a camera opening without warning. Guarantors would otherwise scroll past someone else’s business.
  • What I did: The frame guides the face and the step tracker stays in view. The applicant adds each guarantor; the guarantor gets a separate link with a much shorter job — verify and sign.
  • Why it mattered: Even the strangest step shows how far you’ve come, and each person only sees the steps that are theirs.

4. The review screen became a repair tool

  • Problem identified: By the end, the application holds around twenty sections. One mistake found late usually means starting again.
  • What I did: The review opens with a summary — loan amount, applicant, business, tenure — then lists every section with a status. Done shows a tick; one that needs attention is flagged amber, one tap from being fixed.
  • Where it landed: The same structure lets an RM send back one section for correction, instead of the whole application.

5. It grew from nine steps to thirteen — without feeling longer

  • Problem identified: Later sprints kept adding sections to a journey first grouped into nine steps.
  • What I did: Let the tracker grow to thirteen, but kept the counter: every screen still says which step you’re on, out of how many.
  • Why it mattered: A longer journey never feels open-ended when you always know where the end is.

One application, three people

The applicant follows one path. The guarantor and the RM join it from the side.

The bank keeps its performance data in-house, so I can’t show completion rates. What I can show is what shipped: a regulated, multi-party loan journey, designed in a month and now the bank’s digital route for business-loan applications.

Built to be measured. Every section reports its own status, so completion, time to submit and resubmissions can all be tracked step by step.

What I would do differently next time

This was a month-long project with almost no room to iterate after handoff. Working alone meant every decision was mine to defend, which pushed me to write down the why behind each one.

Test the two risky steps with real owners. I’d push for an hour with business owners on the split OTP and the statement upload — the two steps I’d expect people to stumble on, and the two I designed without watching anyone use them.
